Sanyi: Remains of the Longteng Bridge
Historical Overview
HISTORY

For ninety years, the Mountain Line Railway has wound its way through the mountainous regions, passing through Taiwan's 
major cities and lands rich in natural resources. It has facilitated the rapid flow of cultural wisdom and the mutual 
exchange of goods between urban and rural industries. Through this process, a unified spatial, historical, and national 
perspective of the island has gradually taken shape. The railway bears witness to Taiwan's historical transformations, 
while the interplay between the tracks and their surrounding environment has further enriched the scenic landscapes of 
Sanyi, Tai'an, Huashan, and the surrounding plateaus. In days gone by, southbound trains would enter this small mountain 
station with a heavy, measured pace. Then, with a lighter step, they would pass through the "Kaitian" Tunnel to reach Signal 
Station No. 167—a junction for passing trains. In an instant, they would arrive at the bridge reputed to be the highest 
in all of Taiwan (standing 33 meters tall): the Xuteng Bridge. This structure is a replacement bridge, rebuilt in 1935 
following the devastating Guandaoshan Earthquake in Taichung. To its east lie the weathered, red-brick ruins of the original 
bridge—the "Longteng Broken Bridge" (originally named the Yutengping Broken Bridge)—widely regarded as the premier scenic 
attraction of the Old Mountain Line. Hailed as a "masterpiece of Taiwanese railway artistry," this broken bridge was 
commissioned in 1905 and completed in 1906. Spanning a total length of 200 meters, the original structure consisted of 
a series of brick arches and piers constructed in segmented sections. It stretched across the Hanshen Stream—a tributary 
of the Jingshan Stream—towering 50 meters above the riverbed below, creating a truly magnificent and imposing spectacle. 
At the time, constructing a bridge capable of supporting the weight of passing trains was indeed a monumental challenge; 
remarkably, the piers of this bridge were built without a single steel reinforcing bar or a single bag of cement—relying 
solely on red bricks and... The granite blocks used in its construction were bonded together with lime mortar—a technique 
designed to absorb and counterbalance the vibrations generated by passing trains. It was this exceptional standard of 
engineering and the bridge's elegant geometric aesthetics that established the reputation of the now-famous Longteng Broken 
Bridge. In the early hours of April 21, 1935, the magnitude 7.3 Guandaoshan Earthquake struck. With its epicenter located 
just five kilometers from the Longteng Broken Bridge, the tremor shook the very foundations of the earth. In a mere few 
dozen seconds, not a single structure survived among the two hundred households in the Longtengping area—a devastation 
that, of course, included the Longteng Broken Bridge itself, which had until then been considered the most robust structure 
of its kind. Following the disaster, in an urgent effort to restore north-south transportation links, a new railway bridge 
was constructed 60 meters to the west of the original site, as the damaged bridge was deemed irreparable.
From the remnants that survive today—specifically, the six dilapidated brick piers at the northern end and the four at the 
southern end—one can discern the ingenuity embedded within the design. According to oral accounts from local railway veterans 
of the older generation, prior to constructing the bridge, the Japanese authorities first established a brickworks nearby to 
ensure a ready and proximate supply of building materials. Given the strict quality standards imposed by the Japanese 
administration at the time, the surviving bricks remain—to this day—among the finest examples of brick craftsmanship. This 
exceptional quality is particularly evident in the stacking patterns of the arched sections and the precision of their angled 
joints, which showcase the exquisite sophistication of the masonry techniques employed during that era.
Regarding the masonry techniques themselves, the construction was heavily influenced by the prevailing sociopolitical climate 
of the time—specifically, the Japanese government's vigorous and comprehensive drive toward Westernization—which led to the 
adoption of the "Dutch Bond" method. Additionally, flanking the base of the arched piers on both sides, one can observe 
clusters of five cubic granite blocks. These slightly protruding granite blocks served a structural purpose during the 
construction phase: they acted as supports for the scaffolding and formwork required to cast the bridge's arches. Furthermore, 
at the time of its completion, this bridge held the distinction—within the Taiwan Railway Department (under Japanese colonial 
rule)—of being the highest-elevation railway bridge with the longest single span (61 meters).
Today, visitors can still view the surviving structural remnants—the six piers at the northern end and the four at the 
southern end. Whether observing the fractured sections of the bridge or the graceful curves of its arches... Whether gazing 
toward the new bridge or focusing on the tung blossoms blanketing the hillsides around the ruined bridge, these spots serve 
as prime locations for railway photography. On September 21, 1999 (Year 88 of the Republic of China), the site once again 
suffered the merciless devastation of the 921 Earthquake; specifically, the fifth arch—counting from the northern end—was 
severed for the second time. Beyond its profound rustic beauty, the "Broken Bridge of Love" stands as a living testament to 
having endured two major earthquakes.
